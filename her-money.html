Her Money

Women in the UK can now do every job, own every kind of property and vote on equal terms. So why do they still end up with less money than men, especially by the time they retire? This page follows the money through a woman's life, and ends with what you can do about it.

How we got here

For most of history, the law treated a married woman's money as her husband's. Many of the rules that held women back financially changed within the lifetime of your grandparents.

    1. The pay gap

    The gender pay gap compares the typical hourly pay of all women and all men. It is not the same as being paid less for the same job, which has been illegal since the Equal Pay Act of 1970. Instead, it shows that women are more often in lower-paid jobs and less often in senior ones.

    For full-time workers the gap has fallen from 17.4% in 1997 to 6.9% in 2025. Women in full-time jobs earned a typical £18.87 an hour, compared with £20.27 for men.

    The gap is much bigger once part-time work is included, because part-time jobs usually pay less per hour. About 4 in 10 working women work part time, compared with about 1 in 7 men.

    Sources: ONS, Gender pay gap in the UK: 2025; earlier ONS releases as reported by the Press Association (2018, 2019, 2023).

    2. The cost of having children

    Before they have children, women and men earn fairly similar amounts. The big split comes after the first baby. Economists call this the child penalty or motherhood penalty.

    Researchers at the Institute for Fiscal Studies followed thousands of British parents. Mothers' hourly pay didn't drop straight away. Instead, it stopped growing: mothers who switched to part-time work missed out on the pay rises and promotions that come with experience. Fathers' pay kept climbing.

    By the time their first child turns 20, mothers have on average spent 4 fewer years in paid work than fathers. Those missing years also mean missing pension savings, which is why the pension gap below is so large.

    Fewer, later babies

    In 2024, women in England and Wales had on average 1.41 children, the lowest on record, and the average mother was 31. People give many reasons for having children later or having fewer, including the cost of housing and childcare. From 2024–25, working parents in England became able to get many more hours of free childcare, and the average cost of a part-time nursery place for a child under two fell by more than half.

    Sources: Institute for Fiscal Studies, The gender wage gap (Costa Dias, Elming, Joyce, 2016); ONS, Births in England and Wales: 2024; Coram, Childcare Survey 2025.

    3. Pensions: where the gaps add up

    A pension is money you save while you work so you have an income when you are old. Every year of lower pay, part-time work or time off adds up, and money in a pension also grows over time, so early years count the most.

    By the time they are 55 to 59, women typically have £81,000 in private pensions and men £156,000, a gap of 48%. Turned into a yearly income, that is roughly £6,000 for women against £11,000 for men. Including people with no private pension at all, the gap is 62%.

    The gap is much smaller for young workers: about 22% at ages 25 to 29. It grows as women take time out for children.

    This is a simplified example in today's money, not financial advice. It assumes the legal minimum workplace pension: 8% of earnings between £6,240 and £50,270 a year (at least 3% from the employer). Pay rises 1.5% a year above inflation while you work but does not grow during a break, and part-time pay is the full-time salary times your hours. Savings grow 3% a year above inflation. Real pensions, pay and investment returns vary.

    The State Pension

    On top of any workplace pension, the government pays a State Pension. In 2025–26 the full amount is £230.25 a week. To get it all you need 35 years of National Insurance contributions or credits; with fewer than 10 you get nothing. If you stay at home to look after a child under 12, you can still get credits, but only if you claim Child Benefit. You can claim and choose not to receive the payments, for example if you or your partner earn over £60,000 and would pay the money back in tax.

    Sources: Department for Work and Pensions, The gender pensions gap in private pensions (2023) and the 2025 research report published with the Pensions Commission; Age UK, State Pension factsheet 2025–26; GOV.UK guidance on Child Benefit and National Insurance credits; HM Treasury, High Income Child Benefit Charge threshold (2024).

    4. Divorce

    When a couple splits up, the person who earned less, or who stepped back from work to look after children, is usually hit harder. That is more often the woman.

    In a 2025 survey of divorced people, women said their income fell by about half in the year after divorce, compared with about 30% for men. About 1 in 4 women said they were struggling financially, against 1 in 6 men.

    Pensions are often forgotten. Since 2000, courts in England and Wales have been able to split a pension between divorcing partners. Yet in one study of 369 divorce settlements, 80% involved at least one pension, but only 14% included an order to share it.

    Sources: Legal & General, The Divorce Gap (2025, survey by Opinium); Woodward and Sefton, Pensions on Divorce (Cardiff University, Nuffield Foundation, 2014).

    5. Wealth: what you own

    Wealth is everything you own (savings, pensions, property and investments) minus what you owe. It builds up over a lifetime, so all the gaps above end up here.

    Men in Great Britain have on average £92,762 more wealth than women, a gap of 35%. The gap is small for people aged 25 to 34 and grows to about 42% by age 64.

    The kind of wealth differs too. More than half of women's wealth is property and possessions, often shared with a partner, while men hold far more pensions and investments. Men own nearly three times as much in UK shares as women.

    Source: Women's Budget Group analysis of the ONS Wealth and Assets Survey, April 2018 to March 2020 (published 2023).

    What you can do

    None of this is fixed. Most of it comes from choices that can be planned for, and some from rules worth knowing early.

    • Stay in your workplace pension

      From age 22, if you earn over £10,000 a year, your employer must put you in a pension and add at least 3% of your pay. If you opt out, you give up that free money.

    • Start early

      Money saved in your twenties has the longest time to grow. Use the calculator above: the early years make the biggest difference.

    • Know what jobs pay

      Look up salaries before you apply, ask about pay rises and promotions, and compare job offers. Big employers publish their gender pay gaps online every year.

    • Share the caring

      Since 2015, parents can share parental leave. When both parents take time off and both work flexibly, neither loses all the pay progression.

    • Claim Child Benefit

      If you ever stay at home with a child under 12, claim Child Benefit so your State Pension keeps building, even if you opt out of the payments.

    • Keep pensions in the picture

      If a relationship ends, pensions can be shared. Check your own State Pension forecast for free on GOV.UK.

    About this page

    This page is for teenagers and young adults. It explains averages for the whole UK; every person's situation is different, and nothing here is financial advice. Figures were checked against the latest published sources in October 2026, and each section lists where its numbers come from.
